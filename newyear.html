笠地蔵

The Six Snow Hats

Japan · Ōmisoka, the last night of the year

In a village by the sea lived an old man and an old woman so poor that, on the last day of the year, there was not a single grain of rice for their New Year cakes.

“I will weave straw hats,” said the old man, “and sell them in town.” He wove five, tied them on a pole and set out through the falling snow.

But the town was busy with its own good fortune, and no one wanted a straw hat. Evening came. The river turned white, the boats went home, and the old man went home too, with all five hats still on his pole.

At the edge of the pine wood stood six stone Jizō, guardians of travellers, their bald heads heaped with snow. “You must be cold,” said the old man, and brushed them clean, and put a hat on each.

Five hats – six Jizō. So he took off his own worn towel and tied it on the last one. Then he walked home bareheaded, and the old woman said, “You did well.”

At midnight, all over Japan, temple bells ring 108 times – one stroke for each worldly worry, so the year begins clean.

Before dawn they heard singing in the snow – and something heavy set down at their door. Zushin!

Outside lay rice, and mochi, and sake, and pine for the gate, and bright cloth for the New Year. And far off down the white road, six small figures in straw hats were walking away – the last one in a towel.

Befana

The Old Woman Who Swept

Italy · the Eve of Epiphany, 5 January

In a house with a balcony lived an old woman who loved one thing above all: a clean floor. She swept in the morning and swept at night, and her broom was the best-known broom in all the hills.

One winter evening three travellers knocked – kings, by their cloaks, all gold and thread. “We follow a star to a newborn child,” they said. “Come with us.”

“Not tonight,” said the old woman. “I have sweeping to do.” And she closed the door.

By midnight she was sorry. She took her broom and a sack of sweets and ran after the star – but the kings were gone and the star was gone, and she has been searching ever since.

Every year on the night of 5 January, La Befana flies over Italy on her broom and stops at every house with a child in it, just in case. Good children find sweets in their stocking. Naughty ones find coal – though it is usually sugar coal, because she is not as stern as she looks.

年

The Beast Called Year

China · Chúxī, the eve of the Spring Festival

Long ago a beast named Nián lived at the bottom of the sea. Once a year, on the last night of winter, it climbed out to eat the crops, the cattle – and anyone it found.

So every year the villagers fled to the mountains. One year an old beggar came and asked to stay. Everyone was packing; only an old grandmother gave him a bowl of rice. “Let me keep your house tonight,” he said, “and I will send the beast away.”

At midnight Nián came down the road. But the grandmother’s door was pasted with red paper, candles burned in every window, and in the yard the old man had thrown bamboo on the fire – pī-pā! pī-pā! – it cracked like thunder.

The beast, it turns out, was afraid of three things: the colour red, bright light, and loud noise. It ran back to the sea and never came again.

And that is why, on New Year’s Eve, the doors are red, the lanterns are lit and the firecrackers never stop. Chase it away yourself:
